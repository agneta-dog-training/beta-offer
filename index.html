You love your dog.

I want to start there, because it's the part most of the advice skips clean over. You didn't get a dog to win at obedience. You got a dog to be part of your family. To share your life with. To bring along on big adventures, and to cuddle on the couch while you watch your comfort show for the fourth time. Your dog is the world to you. But the hard days are really hard.

So you've rearranged your life around the hard days. Who comes over. Which routes you walk and when. How long you can be out of the house.

And underneath all of it is something that feels a lot like grief. You love your dog enormously, and you're also mourning the dog you thought you'd have, and the life you pictured together. Then comes the guilt for feeling that at all. And on top of the guilt, the worry about what everyone else thinks. Your relatives who say you baby them. Your neighbour who hand-delivers unsolicited training advice. And that random-ass person on the street who asks why you'd ever get that kind of dog anyway, they're so high-energy.

Some days this has you wishing they were a different dog. But you don't want a different dog. You want this one, minus the exhaustion.

  • 🐾You want to clip on the leash and walk out the door without scanning the whole street and bracing for who you might see.
  • 🐾You want to take them to the vet when they need to go, not when you just can't put it off any longer.
  • 🐾You want to trim their nails before they curl under.
  • 🐾You want to enjoy your dog, the goofy, sweet dog you know better than anyone else.
  • 🐾You want more easy days than hard ones.

And I know you've tried.

You read the books, the good ones, and maybe one that promised a brand new version of your dog in six weeks and delivered a slightly more confused version of you. You hired a trainer. Or two. Or three. You did the training, and it worked, in the sense that your dog can absolutely sit, lie down, go to mat, and perform a small repertoire of delightful party tricks. You typed your exact problem into Google and YouTube and a Facebook group and ChatGPT and got back a heap of confident, contradictory advice.

Some of it helped, a little. Most of it didn't. And a couple things might have made it worse, which you then filed away as more evidence that the problem must be you. Or that something is really wrong with your dog.

What that advice never tells you is that behaviour is genuinely, maddeningly complicated, and most of the people selling you a fix have a financial interest in pretending it's simple.

Stay with me for a minute. The behaviour isn't the problem. It's the clue that points to it.

I know how tempting it is to narrow in on the behaviour itself. But turning off a smoke alarm doesn't get rid of the smoke. Clear the smoke, and the alarm goes quiet on its own.

That reframe is the difference between corralling your dog for the rest of their life, whether with a carrot or a stick, and actually shifting their behaviour in a way that feels good for both of you.

And it's almost entirely missing from the advice you've been drowning in, because "make the symptom stop" fits in a twenty-second TikTok, and "let's investigate what's really going on with your dog" doesn't even begin to.

I'm a dog trainer and behaviour specialist. My approach is force free and positive reinforcement based, meaning I don't use methods that rely on pain, fear, or intimidation.

I work with the behaviour issues that make ordinary days harder than they need to be: barking, growling, fear and anxiety, over-excitement, leash pulling, the recall that only works in the kitchen, the vet and grooming visits that go sideways.

But before I got here, I was you. Crying in my bedroom, exhausted, over a dog I loved and couldn't figure out.
