You love your dog.

I want to start there, because it's the part most of the advice skips clean over. You didn't get a dog to win at obedience. You got a dog to be a part of your family. To share your life with. To bring along on big adventures, and to cuddle on the couch while you watch your comfort show for the fourth time. Your dog is the world to you. But the hard days are really hard.

So you've rearranged your life around the hard days. Who comes over. Which routes you walk and when. How long you can be out of the house.

And underneath all of it is something that feels a lot like grief. You love your dog enormously, and you're also mourning the dog you thought you'd have, and the life you'd pictured together. Then comes the guilt for feeling that at all. And on top of the guilt, the worry about what everyone else thinks. Your relatives who say you baby them. Your neighbour who hand-delivers unsolicited training advice. And that random-ass person on the street who asks why you'd ever get that kind of dog anyway, they're so high-energy.

Some days this has you wishing they were a different dog. But you don't want a different dog. You want this one, minus the exhaustion.

  • You want to clip on the leash and walk out the door without scanning the whole street and bracing for who you might see.
  • You want to take them to the vet when they need to go, not when you just can't put it off any longer.
  • You want to trim their nails before they curl under.
  • You want to enjoy your dog, the goofy, sweet dog you know better than anyone else.
  • You want more easy days than hard ones.

And I know you've tried.

You typed your exact problem into Google and YouTube and a Facebook group and ChatGPT and got back a heap of confident, contradictory advice. You scrolled through all the top-rated dog training podcasts and downloaded about fifty episodes for your daily commute. You even read that one book that promised a brand new version of your dog in six weeks and delivered a slightly more confused version of you. And then you hired a trainer. Or two. Or three. You did the training and it worked, in the sense that your dog can absolutely sit, lie down, go to mat, and perform a small repertoire of delightful party tricks.

Some of it helped, a little. Most of it didn't. And a couple things might have made it worse, which you then filed away as more evidence that the problem must be you. Or that something is really wrong with your dog.

What none of the advice tells you is that behaviour is genuinely, maddeningly complicated, and most of the people selling you a fix have a financial interest in pretending it's simple.

Stay with me for a minute. The behaviour isn't the problem. It's the clue that points to the problem.

I know how tempting it is to narrow in on the behaviour itself. But turning off a smoke alarm doesn't get rid of the smoke. Clear the smoke, and the alarm goes quiet on its own.

That reframe is the difference between corralling your dog for the rest of their life, whether with a carrot or a stick, and actually shifting their behaviour in a way that feels good for both of you.

And it's almost entirely missing from the advice you've been drowning in, because "make the symptom stop" fits in a twenty-second TikTok, and "let's investigate what's really going on with your dog" doesn't even begin to.

After booking, you'll complete an intake covering your dog's history, the behaviour issues, and your goals, so I arrive at our first session already knowing your situation.

I'll ask follow-up questions to pinpoint what's really driving your dog's behaviour, then we'll map out the bigger-picture strategy together, prioritized around your goals and what your dog is actually ready for. And because even the best plans need adjusting, we'll troubleshoot and tweak as we go, making sure it stays effective and realistic for your actual life. You're never left to figure it out alone.

Questions you might have.

How is this different from the trainers I've already worked with?

Training teaches skills, and skills matter a lot! But behaviour issues usually involve more than a gap in skills, especially when there are big feelings involved, like with reactivity, fear, anxiety, aggression, or even overexcitement.

Those cases need support that looks at the whole picture and addresses what's actually driving the behaviour, instead of just drilling training exercises and wondering why nothing is shifting.

Most of my clients come to me having already worked with at least one other trainer. They've usually made some progress and then their dog's behaviour plateaued, or started sliding backward.

There are two main things I tend to do differently. First, I assess what else is likely driving the behaviour, whether that's emotional, physical, medical, or psychiatric, and I work alongside your veterinarian, veterinary behaviourist, canine physiotherapist, etc. where needed.

Second, you get support between sessions. Most behaviour work happens when I'm not there, and that's exactly when the plan meets reality and things risk going sideways. Feedback in that moment, rather than weeks later, changes what's possible.

Can I book just a single session, or extend past four months?

I don't offer single sessions to new clients, and here's the honest why. The cases I take on are complex enough that they need ongoing support to get you where you want to go.

Behaviour change needs time, consistency, practice, and support, and in my experience this package length and structure is ideal for making real, lasting progress on most behaviour concerns.

If your dog's behaviour can genuinely be sorted in a session or two, that's great news! It also means I'm not your best fit, and I'll happily point you toward someone who is.

Some complex cases, or dogs with several behaviour concerns, may benefit from continued work past the four months. Flexible ongoing support is available at a reduced rate for existing clients.

How soon will I see results, and can you guarantee them?

Any effective intervention should produce some shift fairly early. If we're not seeing that, it's my signal to troubleshoot or pivot. You won't find yourself months in, wondering when something is going to change.

That said, how long it takes to solve your dog's behaviour issues depends on how complex your dog's case is, your own training skillset, how much time and consistent practice you can realistically give it, and how much back-and-forth is needed with your vet for cases with a medical component.

I can't guarantee specific outcomes, because behaviour is not guaranteeable. Behaviour depends on genetics, history, health, environment, and a dozen other things neither of us fully controls.

What I do promise is full transparency, a rigorous behaviour change plan that is grounded in the behaviour sciences, and staying with you through it. In the vast majority of cases there's room for meaningful improvement, and I'll work with you to find solutions that are practical, sustainable, and feel good for both you and your dog.

Are we going to change my dog's behaviour, or just manage it?

Both, and management isn't the compromise it sounds like. I think the worry comes from hearing management described as a bandaid, but good management is behaviour science applied proactively. It increases what you and your dog can do rather than shrinking your world, and without it, training alone often fails to produce lasting change.

So the answer is yes to behaviour change and yes to management. They work together.

What training methods do you use?

I'm a force free, positive reinforcement based trainer, working within the modern animal welfare frameworks called LIFE (Least Inhibitive, Functionally Effective) and LIMA (Least Intrusive, Minimally Aversive).

In plain terms: no force, no pain, no fear, no intimidation.

Instead of setting your dog up to make a mistake and then correcting them, I help you set them up to make the right choice in the first place and reward that instead. It sounds like a small distinction, but in practice it's a completely different approach, not just a softer version of the same one.

It's also not a trade-off between kind and effective. Your dog learns to make better choices because they want to, not because they're worried about what will happen if they don't. This kind of root-level behaviour change actually holds up long term, without costing you their trust.

I'm currently using a prong, e-collar, or choke chain. Can we work together?

If you are currently using a prong, e-collar, choke chain, or any other tool or method that is designed to suppress behaviour through the application of physical or psychological pressure, there's no judgement here. Most people use these tools and methods because they were told to do so by someone they trusted.

However, working with me means transitioning off them. I don't teach these tools and methods, and I don't work alongside them. There absolutely are always alternatives, and I stand by that statement.

So, if that is a change you've been wanting to make but have either not known how to or perhaps tried to but were met with judgement (I'm very sorry you experienced that!), I am here to help.

If you're committed to keeping these tools and are entirely certain that your dog needs them, then I'm not the right fit!

I'm exhausted and I don't have much left to give. Can I even do this?

Yes, I build the behaviour change plan around how much you've actually got in the tank, not some fantasy version of you with unlimited time and energy. A plan you can't sustain isn't a good plan. It's just one more thing to feel stressed about. There are many potential solutions to any given problem, and we'll find one that fits your real life.

My only note is that the exact outcome will differ based on the approach. In all cases we can find a solution to the problem, but it will look different if you have a couple hours a week to work on it versus if you have next to no time available in your week. All positive outcomes are valid, just know that there are trade-offs and I can't work magic (unfortunately! I super wish I could though!)
